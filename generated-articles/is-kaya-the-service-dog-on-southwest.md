### Distinguishing Service Animals from Emotional Support Animals (ESAs)

The distinction between service animals and emotional support animals (ESAs) is critical for air travel. Many travelers still confuse these two categories. I want to make sure the difference is very clear. The rules for ESAs have changed significantly, especially for airlines. This affects how they travel on flights like those with Southwest.

A service animal is a dog specifically trained to perform tasks for an individual with a disability. These tasks must be directly related to the person's disability. Examples include guiding the blind or alerting to medical conditions. This specialized training is the defining characteristic of a service animal. The Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) protects service animals. The Air Carrier Access Act (ACAA) also outlines their rights on airplanes.

Emotional support animals, on the other hand, provide comfort through their presence. They do not have specialized task training for a disability. While they offer significant therapeutic benefits, their role is different. The U.S. Department of Transportation (DOT) revised its rules in 2021. This revision removed ESAs from the definition of service animals under the ACAA. This means airlines are no longer required to treat ESAs as service animals.

Southwest Airlines, like most major carriers, no longer accepts ESAs as service animals. If you wish to travel with an emotional support animal, it must now travel as a pet. This means it will be subject to pet fees and size restrictions. It must also travel in a carrier that fits under the seat in front of you. This policy change has impacted many travelers who previously flew with their ESAs.

Understanding this difference is important for planning your trip. If your dog is a service animal, it must meet the DOT's definition and requirements. This includes submitting the necessary forms. If your dog is an ESA, it will travel under pet rules. Always confirm the current policies directly with Southwest before booking. This helps avoid any surprises or disruptions at the airport.

### Legal Frameworks: ADA and ACAA for Service Animals in Air Travel

Two main acts establish the rights and responsibilities: the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) and the Air Carrier Access Act (ACAA). These laws work together to ensure accessibility for individuals with disabilities.

The Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) broadly defines a service animal. It protects the rights of individuals with disabilities to use service animals in public places. This includes businesses, government agencies, and transportation facilities. The ADA defines a service animal as a dog individually trained to do work or perform tasks for a person with a disability. These tasks must directly relate to the person's disability. This general definition forms the foundation for airline policies.

For air travel specifically, the Air Carrier Access Act (ACAA) is the primary federal law. The U.S. Department of Transportation (DOT) enforces the ACAA. This act prohibits discrimination against individuals with disabilities in air travel. It includes specific provisions for service animals. The ACAA requires airlines to accommodate service animals. It also sets out rules for what airlines can and cannot ask or require.

A key component under the ACAA is the U.S. Department of Transportation (DOT) Service Animal Air Transportation Form. This form serves as the primary documentation for service animals on flights. Handlers must affirm the animal's health, training, and behavior. They must also affirm that the animal performs tasks for a disability. This form helps streamline the verification process for airlines. It reduces the need for other types of documentation.

The ACAA also clarifies that airlines cannot impose breed restrictions on service animals. They also cannot demand specific identification, like vests. However, airlines can deny boarding if a service animal poses a direct threat. They can also deny if the animal causes a significant disruption.
