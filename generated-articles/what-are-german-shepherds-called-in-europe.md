### What are German Shepherds called in Europe?

In Europe, the German Shepherd is primarily known by its original German name, *Deutscher Schäferhund*. However, in countries like the United Kingdom and Ireland, the historical name "Alsatian" is still sometimes used. This reflects a period when the "German" descriptor was removed for political reasons. Today, "German Shepherd Dog" or *Deutscher Schäferhund* is common across most of the continent, especially within official breed circles.

### The Original Name: Deutscher Schäferhund

The German Shepherd breed originated in Germany. Captain Max von Stephanitz created this breed in the late 19th century. He aimed to develop a superior herding dog. He succeeded in his goal of creating a national working breed.

The name he gave the breed was *Deutscher Schäferhund*. This translates directly to "German Shepherd Dog." It perfectly describes the breed's heritage and purpose. This name emphasizes its German origin and its primary function as a sheepdog.

This original designation is still the official name recognized by the breed club in Germany, the SV (Verein für Deutsche Schäferhunde). The SV is the oldest and largest German Shepherd Dog club in the world. Its standards influence the breed globally. The SV works tirelessly to preserve the breed's original traits and working ability.

When you speak to breeders in Germany, they will always use *Deutscher Schäferhund*. It is a matter of pride and historical accuracy for them. They value this specific terminology. This name connects the dogs directly to their German roots and their founder.

### The "Alsatian" Debate: A Historical Overview

The name "Alsatian" for German Shepherds has a very interesting history. It stems from political tensions during the 20th century. World War I saw a rise in anti-German sentiment in many Allied countries. People wanted to distance themselves from anything German.

Dog breeds with "German" in their name faced changes. The German Shepherd Dog was one of them. In the UK, the Kennel Club officially changed the breed's name to "Alsatian Wolf Dog" in 1917. This was later shortened to just "Alsatian." The term Alsatian comes from Alsace, a region located on the French-German border. This region has changed hands between Germany and France many times throughout history.

This name change aimed to make the breed more palatable to the public. It helped reduce negative associations with Germany during wartime. The name change stuck for decades in several English-speaking countries. It was only in 1977 that the UK Kennel Club reverted the name to "German Shepherd Dog." This decision came after much campaigning by breed enthusiasts. They believed the original name should be restored.

Even today, you might hear older generations in the UK and Ireland refer to German Shepherds as Alsatians. It is a lingering piece of history. The debate over the name highlights how external events can shape breed identity. The breed's popularity never wavered, regardless of its name.

### Understanding European Breed Standards: FCI and SV Influence

European breed standards for German Shepherds are largely influenced by two major organizations. The Fédération Cynologique Internationale (FCI) is the leading international canine organization. The SV (Verein für Deutsche Schäferhunde) is the German national club. These bodies work together to set benchmarks for the breed.

The FCI recognizes *Deutscher Schäferhund* as the official name. It has 99 member countries, including most of Europe. The FCI standard outlines the ideal physical characteristics and temperament. These include coat color, height, weight, and gait. The standard also stresses the importance of working ability and sound health. European breeders often follow these precise guidelines.

The SV, based in Germany, has a profound impact. It is the parent club for the breed. The SV sets the original and most detailed standards for the German Shepherd. Their standards are very strict. They focus heavily on maintaining the breed's working qualities. This includes strong nerves, courage, and intelligence. The SV conducts rigorous health checks. These checks include hip and elbow evaluations.

Many European breeders prioritize SV certifications. They value these certifications because they ensure quality and adherence to traditional breed goals. The SV also organizes major competitions and shows. These events help promote the ideal German Shepherd. They keep the breed's integrity strong.

These unified standards mean that a German Shepherd bred in France should largely conform to the same ideals as one bred in Germany. This helps maintain consistency across the continent. It ensures the breed's health and characteristics. These standards also define what makes a German Shepherd recognizable worldwide.

### Working Line vs. Show Line: European Perspectives

In Europe, the distinction between "working line" and "show line" German Shepherds is very clear. This separation impacts breeding goals and the dogs' primary purpose. Both lines are still *Deutscher Schäferhund*, but they serve different roles. I find this distinction essential for understanding the breed.

**Working Line German Shepherds**

*   Working line German Shepherds are bred specifically for performance. Their traits include intelligence, drive, and physical stamina. They excel in protection sports, search and rescue, and police work.
*   Breeders prioritize temperament, health, and working aptitude above all else. Their appearance is secondary. They often have less extreme angulation than show lines. This build supports sustained physical activity.
*   These dogs are common in police and military units across Europe. Their strong nerves and focus make them ideal for demanding tasks. They also have a very high drive.
*   Training a working line dog requires significant commitment. They need a job to do. Without proper stimulation, they can become bored or destructive. This is because they have so much energy.

**Show Line German Shepherds**

*   Show line German Shepherds are bred primarily for conformation to breed standards in appearance. Their physical beauty and gait are paramount. They have more exaggerated features.
*   They often exhibit a more sloped back and extreme angulation in their hindquarters. This gives them a characteristic "flying trot" gait. This look is favored in beauty competitions.
*   While they still possess intelligence and good temperament, their drive for work is generally lower. They are excellent family companions. They fit well into homes that cannot provide intense working stimulation.
*   There are both West German Show Lines and East German Show Lines. West German lines often feature the sloped back. East German lines (DDR) are closer to working lines in appearance and drive. They were bred for border patrol.

The choice between a working line and a show line depends on an owner's lifestyle. It also depends on their goals for the dog. Both types are true German Shepherds. However, their genetic focus is quite different. The differences are important for potential owners to understand.
