Cherry eye happens when the third eyelid gland prolapses, or pops out. This gland is vital for producing tears. When it pops out, it looks like a small red cherry in the corner of your dog's eye. ## Understanding Cherry Eye: What is This Eye Condition?

Cherry eye is a common condition in dogs. It looks like a small, red lump in the inner corner of a dog's eye. This lump is actually the dog's third eyelid gland. Dogs have an extra eyelid called the nictitating membrane. This membrane contains a gland that produces a significant portion of the dog's tears. When this gland pops out of its normal position, it causes cherry eye.

The gland becomes swollen and visible. It loses its proper anchoring. This makes it look like a cherry. The condition can affect one eye or both. It can also appear at different times. Many dog owners feel worried when they first see it. It is important to know this is a treatable condition.

### What Causes Cherry Eye in Dogs?

The exact cause of cherry eye is not always clear. However, it often relates to weak connective tissue. This tissue holds the gland in place. When this tissue weakens, the gland can prolapse. Certain dog breeds are more likely to get cherry eye. These breeds include Beagles, Bulldogs, Cocker Spaniels, and Boston Terriers. Brachycephalic breeds, with their flatter faces, also have a higher risk. You can learn more about specific triggers by exploring [what is the cause of cherry eye in dogs](https://beacleaner.com/what-is-the-cause-of-cherry-eye-in-dogs).

Sometimes, trauma or irritation can contribute. But usually, it is a genetic predisposition. Puppies and young dogs are most commonly affected. This happens because their connective tissues are still developing. The condition usually appears before two years of age. It is not something you can prevent with diet or exercise.

### Recognizing the Signs of Cherry Eye

The main sign of cherry eye is the visible red mass. It appears in the inner corner of the eye. Other signs include increased tearing. Your dog might rub or paw at their eye. The eye may also look red or irritated. Sometimes, dogs develop a secondary eye infection. This infection happens due to increased exposure of the gland.

The exposed gland cannot produce tears properly. This leads to dryness and irritation. If left untreated, the gland can suffer permanent damage. This damage leads to chronic dry eye. Dry eye is a painful condition. It requires lifelong management. If you see these signs, contact your vet quickly.

## Why Consider Cherry Eye Treatment: Benefits of Intervention

When your dog has cherry eye, acting quickly matters. The main reason to treat cherry eye is to protect your dog's vision. The prolapsed gland affects tear production. Tears keep the eye moist and healthy. Without enough tears, the eye becomes dry. This leads to pain and irritation. It also raises the risk of serious eye diseases.

Treating cherry eye prevents these problems. It helps your dog live a more comfortable life. A dog with untreated cherry eye often suffers. They have ongoing irritation. They may experience blurry vision or even blindness over time. Early treatment avoids these severe outcomes. This makes intervention a very good choice.

### Preventing Dry Eye Syndrome

The third eyelid gland produces up to 60% of a dog's tears. When this gland is exposed, it dries out. This reduces its ability to make tears. The result is a condition called Keratoconjunctivitis Sicca (KCS), or "dry eye." Dry eye is a painful and chronic condition. It requires daily eye drops and lifelong care. This care can be costly and time-consuming.

Surgical repositioning of the gland helps restore normal tear function. This significantly reduces the risk of dry eye. Preventing dry eye protects your dog's comfort. It also saves you from the burden of long-term medical treatment. This is a major benefit of treating cherry eye.

### Avoiding Chronic Irritation and Pain

An exposed cherry eye gland causes constant irritation. The gland rubs against the environment. It collects dirt and debris. This leads to redness, swelling, and discomfort. Your dog may paw at their eye frequently. They might squint or show signs of pain. This ongoing irritation affects their quality of life.

Treating the cherry eye eliminates this source of irritation. It puts the gland back where it belongs. This stops the rubbing and exposure. Your dog will feel much better after treatment. They can enjoy their daily activities without eye pain. This improves their overall well-being.

### What Happens If I Don't Fix My Dog's Cherry Eye?

Ignoring cherry eye can lead to serious health issues. The exposed gland is vulnerable to damage and infection. Chronic inflammation can permanently harm the gland. ## Surgical Procedures for Cherry Eye: What to Expect

When it comes to treating cherry eye, surgery is the most effective method. The goal of surgery is not to remove the gland. It is to reposition it. Vets use different surgical techniques. All aim to put the gland back into its natural pocket. This helps restore its function. It also reduces future problems.

Your vet will examine your dog. They will discuss the best surgical approach. This decision depends on factors like the size of the gland. It also depends on the chronicity of the condition. Each method has a good success rate. Recovery typically involves some aftercare.

### Common Surgical Techniques

There are two main types of surgery for cherry eye. The "pocket technique" is very common. The vet creates a small pocket behind the third eyelid. Then, they tuck the gland into this pocket. They stitch the pocket closed. This holds the gland in place. This method is effective and preserves the gland.

Another technique is the "orbital rim anchoring" method. Here, the vet stitches the gland to the tissue around the eye socket. This anchors the gland securely. Both methods aim to keep the gland hidden and functional. Your vet chooses the technique they feel is best for your dog's specific case. You can ask them about their preferred method.

### What to Expect During Surgery

Cherry eye surgery is performed under general anesthesia. Your dog will be asleep. The vet prepares the eye area carefully. They ensure it is sterile. The surgery itself usually takes about 30 to 60 minutes. It is a delicate procedure. It requires a skilled veterinary surgeon.

After the surgery, your dog will wake up. They will likely go home the same day or the next. They will have some discomfort. The vet will prescribe pain relief. They will also give anti-inflammatory medications. Sometimes, antibiotics are prescribed to prevent infection. You will also get specific instructions for at-home care.

One possible complication is recurrence. The cherry eye might pop out again. This happens in a small percentage of cases. It can be due to weakened tissue.
