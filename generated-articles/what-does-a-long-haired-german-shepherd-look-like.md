## Long Haired German Shepherd Appearance: What Do They Look Like?

Have you ever seen a German Shepherd with an exceptionally flowing coat and wondered if it was a different breed? You likely saw a long-haired German Shepherd. These dogs capture attention with their stunning, luxurious fur. People often ask me about their unique look.

I find their distinct appearance truly captivating. This article explores the specific traits that define a long-haired German Shepherd. We will look at their coat, size, colors, and other features. By the end, you will easily recognize these magnificent animals.

### Takeaway

*   Long-haired German Shepherds have a distinctive long, flowing outer coat and a minimal or absent undercoat.
*   Their fur often creates feathering around the ears, legs, and tail.
*   They share the same powerful build and loyal temperament as their short-haired counterparts.
*   Coat care is essential to keep their long fur healthy and tangle-free.
*   Their appearance is striking and often mistaken for a different breed.

### Clear, Concise Answer to the Main Query

A long-haired German Shepherd displays a thick, flowing coat of soft, long fur across its body. This coat often feathers around the ears, legs, and tail, giving them a majestic look. They retain the classic German Shepherd build, colors, and intelligent expression, but their coat length defines their unique appearance.

## The Distinctive Long Coat of a German Shepherd

The most striking feature of a long-haired German Shepherd is, naturally, its coat. Unlike the standard German Shepherd, which has a double coat of medium length, the long-haired variant has a significantly longer outer coat. This fur can be soft and flowing, creating a beautiful silhouette. You might notice the hair grows longer around the ears, on the back of the legs, and along the tail.

This longer hair gives them a somewhat softer, more gentle look than their short-haired relatives. The texture of the coat can vary, but it is typically softer to the touch. It often lacks the dense undercoat present in short-haired German Shepherds. This makes their coat feel lighter and more airy. This lack of a thick undercoat can affect their insulation properties in extreme weather.

Recognizing this specific coat is key to identifying a long-haired German Shepherd. When I first learned about them, I found the difference surprising. The long hair often forms noticeable 'feathering' or 'trousers' on their hind legs. It also creates a plume-like tail. This flowing fur is a genetic trait. It makes them stand out in any crowd. People often ask me how to tell if a German Shepherd puppy is long-haired; the longer fur becomes more apparent as they grow. ### Coat Texture and Undercoat

The texture of a long-haired German Shepherd's coat is usually softer than a standard coat. Standard German Shepherds have a coarse outer coat that protects them from elements. The long-haired variety often has a silkier texture. This softness makes them incredibly pleasant to pet.

A significant difference lies in their undercoat. Many long-haired German Shepherds have a greatly reduced or even absent undercoat. The undercoat is the dense, woolly layer beneath the outer fur. It provides insulation against cold and heat. Without a substantial undercoat, these dogs might be more sensitive to temperature extremes. This means they need extra care in very hot or very cold climates. I always recommend owners consider their dog's comfort in all weather.

*   **Soft Outer Coat:** The top layer of fur is long, flowing, and typically soft.
*   **Minimal Undercoat:** Many lack the dense undercoat found in standard GSDs.
*   **Feathering:** Noticeable longer hair on ears, legs, and tail.
*   **Vulnerability:** Less insulation means potential sensitivity to extreme temperatures.

### Common Coat Colors

Long-haired German Shepherds come in the same wide range of colors as their short-haired relatives. The most common color patterns include black and tan. This classic combination features a black saddle over a tan or reddish-brown body. Other popular colors are black and red, which has deeper, richer tan markings. Black and cream is another lovely variation, offering a lighter contrast.

Solid black long-haired German Shepherds are also quite striking. Their long, dark fur looks very elegant. You might also find solid white, sable, or bi-color coats. Sable coats have individual hairs with multiple bands of color. Bi-color coats show a distinct pattern of black with lighter markings. The long fur can sometimes make these colors appear even more vibrant. ## Body Structure and Size of Long Haired German Shepherds

A long-haired German Shepherd maintains the same powerful and athletic build as its short-haired counterpart. They are large, muscular dogs known for their strength and agility. Their body is well-proportioned, reflecting their heritage as working dogs. You will see a robust bone structure and a deep chest. This build allows them to perform demanding tasks with ease.

Their gait is smooth and flowing. This demonstrates their incredible athleticism. They move with purpose and grace. This powerful body is one reason why German Shepherds are so admired. The long hair simply adds a layer of elegance to this already impressive physique. When I watch them move, I always see a balance of power and beauty.

The size of a long-haired German Shepherd falls within the typical range for the breed. Males usually stand between 24 and 26 inches (60-65 cm) at the shoulder. Females are slightly smaller, generally measuring 22 to 24 inches (55-60 cm). Their weight also aligns with breed standards. Males can weigh from 65 to 90 pounds (29-41 kg). Females typically weigh between 50 and 70 pounds (22-32 kg). These are approximations, and individual dogs might vary. ### Head and Facial Features

The head of a long-haired German Shepherd is noble and refined. It is proportionate to their body, not too heavy or too fine. They have a strong, wedge-shaped head with a defined stop. The muzzle is long and straight, tapering to a black nose. Their teeth meet in a strong scissor bite. These features contribute to their alert and intelligent expression.

Their eyes are medium-sized and almond-shaped. They are typically dark brown. The expression in their eyes is often keen, intelligent, and confident. These dogs are always observing their surroundings. Their ears are erect and pointed. They are set high on the head. Puppies might have floppy ears at first, but they usually stand up by a few months old. The long hair around their ears can sometimes give them a softer look, but the ear shape itself remains characteristic of the breed.

*   **Strong, Wedge-Shaped Head:** Proportionate and powerful.
*   **Almond-Shaped Eyes:** Dark brown, showing intelligence and alertness.
*   **Erect, Pointed Ears:** Set high, indicating keen awareness.
*   **Black Nose:** A standard feature, enhancing their serious look.

### Tail and Legs

The tail of a long-haired German Shepherd is a defining feature. It is usually bushy and curves slightly like a saber. The long fur on the tail gives it a magnificent, plume-like appearance. When the dog is relaxed, the tail hangs in a gentle curve. When alert or excited, it can rise, but it should not curl over the back. A tail that curls too much is considered a fault in the breed standard.

Their legs are straight and well-muscled. The front legs are straight when viewed from all sides. The hind legs are strong and angled. This angulation is important for their powerful movement. The long hair often forms 'trousers' or 'feathering' on the back of their hind legs. This extra fur is a clear sign of a long-haired variant. Their feet are compact, with well-arched toes and thick pads. These sturdy feet help them maintain grip and agility on various terrains.

I love watching the sway of their feathered tail as they walk. It adds to their majestic presence. The long fur on their legs also makes them seem even more substantial. It truly completes their regal appearance.

## Temperament and Health of the Long Haired German Shepherd

The temperament of a long-haired German Shepherd is identical to that of its short-haired relatives. They are known for being intelligent, loyal, and confident. These dogs are highly trainable and eager to please their owners. They often form strong bonds with their families. This loyalty makes them excellent companions.

German Shepherds are very versatile. They excel in various roles, from family pets to working dogs. They can be police dogs, search and rescue dogs, or service animals. Their intelligence means they need mental stimulation. This prevents boredom and destructive behaviors. I always tell new owners that these dogs thrive on having a job to do.

Despite their different coat, long-haired German Shepherds share the same potential health concerns as standard German Shepherds. Hip and elbow dysplasia are common orthopedic issues. Responsible breeders screen their dogs for these conditions. Other concerns include degenerative myelopathy and certain eye conditions. Regular veterinary check-ups are essential for their health. I always stress the importance of finding a reputable breeder who prioritizes health.

### The Role of Genetics in Coat Length

The long coat in German Shepherds is due to a recessive gene. This means a puppy needs to inherit a copy of the long-hair gene from both parents to have long hair. If a dog inherits only one copy, it will have a standard coat but can pass the long-hair gene to its offspring. This genetic trait is why long-haired German Shepherds are less common.

The German Shepherd Dog Club of America and some other kennel clubs originally considered long hair a fault. They preferred the standard double coat. However, attitudes have changed over time. Many European kennel clubs now recognize the long-haired German Shepherd as a distinct, purebred variation. They are often shown in separate classes. This recognition highlights their growing acceptance. *   **Recessive Gene:** Two copies needed for long hair.
*   **Breeding:** Both parents must carry the gene.
*   **Club Recognition:** Increasingly accepted by major kennel clubs.
*   **Purebred Status:** They are purebred German Shepherds, not a mixed breed.

### Grooming Requirements for Long Coats

Long-haired German Shepherds require more extensive grooming than their short-haired counterparts. Their flowing coats are prone to tangles and mats if not brushed regularly. Daily or every-other-day brushing is often necessary. This helps remove loose hair, dirt, and prevents knots. A good quality slicker brush and a metal comb are essential tools.

During shedding season, grooming needs increase further. German Shepherds shed throughout the year, but shedding is heavier twice a year. Regular brushing helps manage the shed fur. It also distributes natural oils, keeping the coat healthy and shiny. Neglecting grooming can lead to uncomfortable mats that might require professional intervention. I find that making grooming a positive experience from a young age helps a lot.

*   **Daily Brushing:** Prevents mats and tangles.
*   **Shedding Management:** Helps control loose hair, especially during seasonal sheds.
*   **Tools:** Slicker brush and metal comb are recommended.
*   **Skin Health:** Brushing promotes blood circulation and healthy skin.

Regular grooming also lets you check for skin issues, parasites, or any lumps. This proactive approach helps catch problems early. I always view grooming as a bonding time with my dogs. It is not just about aesthetics; it is about their health and comfort.

## Long Haired German Shepherds vs. Other Shepherd Types

When people see a long-haired German Shepherd, they sometimes confuse it with other similar breeds. One common comparison is with the Belgian Shepherd, particularly the Tervuren or Groenendael varieties. Both have long coats, but there are distinct differences in their build and head shape. Belgian Shepherds are generally more finely built and have a more elegant, refined head. Their ears are often larger and more pointed in proportion to their head.

Another breed sometimes mentioned is the King Shepherd. The King Shepherd is a relatively new breed developed from German Shepherds, Alaskan Malamutes, and Great Pyrenees. King Shepherds are significantly larger and heavier than German Shepherds. They also have a dense, long coat, but their overall mass and bone structure are much more substantial. While a long-haired German Shepherd can look regal, a King Shepherd is truly massive. It is important to remember that a long-haired German Shepherd is still a purebred German Shepherd. The coat length is the primary difference. They retain all the core characteristics of the breed.

### Long Haired vs. Plush Coat German Shepherds

The term "plush coat" German Shepherd can sometimes be used interchangeably with "long-haired," but there's a subtle distinction. A plush coat typically refers to a German Shepherd with a very dense, soft, and slightly longer than average double coat. This coat still has a protective undercoat. The length is usually medium to long, but not as excessively long and flowing as a true long-haired German Shepherd.

A true long-haired German Shepherd, especially those with no undercoat, has hair that is visibly much longer. It feathers significantly around the ears, legs, and tail, creating a very distinct silhouette. The plush coat is more about density and softness, while the long coat is about extreme length. Both are beautiful, but they offer different aesthetics and grooming requirements. I have seen many plush coats, and they are indeed lovely. However, they do not quite match the dramatic look of a genuinely long-haired dog.

*   **Plush Coat:** Dense, soft, medium-to-long double coat.
*   **Long-Haired:** Very long, flowing outer coat, often with minimal undercoat.
*   **Length:** Plush is longer than standard, but long-haired is significantly longer than plush.
*   **Undercoat:** Plush has a good undercoat; long-haired may have little or none.

The long coat develops gradually. At a few weeks old, the difference might be slight. By three to four months, the longer hair becomes much more evident. ### Distinguishing from Standard Coat Puppies

Distinguishing a long-haired puppy from a standard-coated one needs careful observation. A standard-coated puppy might have a plush or fluffy coat in its early months. This can confuse new owners. However, the key difference lies in the *growth* of the hair. A long-haired puppy's fur will consistently be longer and more flowing. It will also lack the coarse texture of a standard GSD puppy's outer coat.

A standard German Shepherd puppy, even with a fluffy appearance, will eventually develop a distinct outer coat and a dense undercoat. The long-haired puppy will continue to grow its softer, longer outer fur, often without a significant undercoat. Examining the hair behind the ears and on the tail provides the clearest indicators. ## Health and Care Considerations for Long Haired German Shepherds

Owning a long-haired German Shepherd comes with specific care considerations, mainly due to their unique coat. As mentioned, their long fur requires consistent grooming to prevent matting and keep it healthy. This is not just for appearance; matted fur can pull on the skin, causing pain and even skin infections. Regular brushing also helps manage shedding, which is a significant factor for any German Shepherd owner.

Their often-reduced undercoat means they may not tolerate extreme temperatures as well as their standard-coated relatives. In hot weather, they can overheat more easily. In cold weather, they might need extra protection. Providing shade, access to water, and avoiding intense activity during peak heat are important. In winter, a coat or sweater might be necessary for extended outdoor exposure.

Like all large breeds, they are prone to certain health issues. Hip and elbow dysplasia are primary concerns. Responsible breeders perform health screenings on parent dogs to reduce the risk of these conditions. Always ask breeders for health clearances for their breeding stock. Regular veterinary check-ups, a balanced diet, and appropriate exercise contribute greatly to their overall well-being. I believe proactive care is the best care for these wonderful dogs.

### Common Health Concerns (Shared with all GSDs)

Long-haired German Shepherds face the same general health risks as all German Shepherds. These include:

*   **Hip and Elbow Dysplasia:** These are genetic conditions affecting the joints. They can lead to arthritis and mobility issues.
*   **Degenerative Myelopathy (DM):** A progressive disease of the spinal cord. It leads to weakness and paralysis in the hind limbs. Genetic testing can identify carriers.
*   **Bloat (Gastric Dilatation-Volvulus):** A life-threatening condition where the stomach twists. Large, deep-chested dogs are at higher risk.
*   **Eye Conditions:** Such as pannus (chronic superficial keratitis) or cataracts.
*   **Allergies:** Some German Shepherds can develop skin or food allergies.

Maintaining a healthy weight, providing a high-quality diet, and ensuring regular exercise help mitigate some of these risks. Early detection through regular vet visits is also important. I always discuss these possibilities with new owners so they are well-informed.

### Exercise and Mental Stimulation Needs

Long-haired German Shepherds are highly intelligent and energetic dogs. They need significant amounts of exercise and mental stimulation to stay happy and well-behaved. Daily walks are not enough. They benefit from activities that challenge both their bodies and minds. This can include:

*   **Long walks or hikes:** Explore new environments.
*   **Jogging or running:** Great for their endurance.
*   **Fetch and other yard games:** Provides physical activity and bonding.
*   **Agility training:** Builds coordination and mental focus.
*   **Obedience training:** Keeps their minds active and reinforces good behavior.
*   **Puzzle toys:** Offers mental challenges when you are not actively engaging them.

A bored German Shepherd can become destructive. They might chew furniture or dig holes. Ensuring they have outlets for their energy is crucial. I find that varied activities keep them engaged and prevent monotony. These dogs thrive when they have a purpose.

## Understanding Breed Standards and Recognition

The recognition of long-haired German Shepherds has evolved significantly over the years. Historically, many major kennel clubs, like the American Kennel Club (AKC), considered the long coat a fault. This meant long-haired GSDs could not compete in conformation shows. They were still purebred but did not meet the preferred coat standard for showing.

However, attitudes began to change, particularly in Europe. Many European kennel clubs, such as the German Shepherd Dog Club of Germany (SV), now recognize the long-haired variety. They are shown in separate classes, allowing them to compete and earn titles. This acceptance acknowledges that the long coat is a natural genetic variation within the breed. It does not signify a mix or a lesser quality dog.

This evolving standard reflects a broader understanding of genetics and breed diversity. For owners, it means that a long-haired German Shepherd is as much a purebred as a short-haired one. The primary difference is simply the aesthetic of their coat. I think this recognition is a positive step. It allows more people to appreciate these beautiful dogs fully.

### The AKC and Other Kennel Clubs

The American Kennel Club (AKC) updated its breed standard for German Shepherds in 2010. This update specified that a "long, soft, open coat is a disqualification." This means while a long-haired German Shepherd is still a purebred and can be registered, it cannot compete in AKC conformation events. However, they can participate in other AKC performance events like obedience, agility, and herding.

In contrast, the Fédération Cynologique Internationale (FCI), which represents many international kennel clubs, recognized the long-haired German Shepherd (Langstockhaar) as a distinct variety in 2011. This allows them to be shown in FCI-affiliated countries. This difference in standards means that the "look" of a show-quality long-haired German Shepherd can vary slightly depending on the country's specific club rules. I advise anyone interested in showing to research their local club's rules.

*   **AKC:** Long coat is a disqualification for conformation, but not for registration or other events.
*   **FCI:** Recognizes long-haired variety, allowing them to compete in conformation shows.
*   **Regional Differences:** Standards vary by country and club.
*   **Purebred Status:** Regardless of show status, they are purebred GSDs.

### Why Some German Shepherds Don't Have Long Hair

The reason some German Shepherds do not have long hair is purely genetic. The gene for long hair is recessive. This means a dog needs two copies of this gene (one from each parent) to express the long coat trait. If a dog has at least one dominant gene for short hair, it will have a short coat, even if it carries the recessive long-hair gene.

This genetic inheritance pattern explains why two short-haired German Shepherds can produce long-haired puppies. If both parents carry the recessive long-hair gene, there is a 25% chance with each puppy that it will inherit two copies of the recessive gene and thus have long hair. This is basic Mendelian genetics. Breeders often use genetic testing to determine if their dogs carry the long-hair gene. ## FAQ Section

### Q1: Is a long-haired German Shepherd a purebred dog?
Yes, a long-haired German Shepherd is a purebred German Shepherd. The long coat is a natural genetic variation within the breed. It results from a recessive gene inherited from both parents. Many international kennel clubs recognize them as a distinct variety of the German Shepherd breed.

### Q2: Do long-haired German Shepherds shed more than short-haired ones?
Long-haired German Shepherds often appear to shed more due to the length of their hair. However, the actual volume of hair shed might be similar to short-haired GSDs. Their long fur is simply more noticeable. Regular grooming helps manage their shedding and prevents mats.

### Q3: Are long-haired German Shepherds harder to train?
No, long-haired German Shepherds are not harder to train. Their intelligence, loyalty, and eagerness to please are identical to short-haired German Shepherds. They respond well to consistent, positive reinforcement training methods. Early socialization and training are key for any German Shepherd.

### Q4: Can a long-haired German Shepherd live in a hot climate?
A long-haired German Shepherd can live in a hot climate, but with extra precautions. Their minimal undercoat means they may be more susceptible to overheating. Provide plenty of shade, fresh water, and avoid strenuous exercise during the hottest parts of the day. Consider cooling mats or vests.

### Q5: What is the main difference between a long-haired and a plush coat German Shepherd?
A long-haired German Shepherd has a very long, flowing outer coat, often with a greatly reduced or absent undercoat, creating noticeable feathering. A plush coat is dense, soft, and slightly longer than a standard coat, but it still has a significant undercoat. The long-haired variety has a more dramatic coat length.
