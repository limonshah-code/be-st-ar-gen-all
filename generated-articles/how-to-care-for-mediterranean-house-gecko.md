## Caring for Your Mediterranean House Gecko

Imagine a tiny, watchful creature with large eyes, scurrying across your wall at dusk. This is often the first encounter people have with a Mediterranean House Gecko. These small lizards are fascinating to observe. Many people discover them in their homes and then wonder about keeping them as pets. While they often live alongside humans in warm climates, caring for a Mediterranean House Gecko as a pet requires understanding their specific needs. This article provides a comprehensive guide to their housing, feeding, environmental controls, and general health, ensuring your little gecko thrives.

### Takeaway

Caring for a Mediterranean House Gecko involves creating a proper enclosure and providing the right diet.
*   Set up a secure terrarium with appropriate substrate and climbing structures.
*   Maintain specific temperature gradients and humidity levels.
*   Offer a varied diet of gut-loaded insects dusted with calcium and vitamins.
*   Monitor their health for signs of common issues like stuck shed or lethargy.
*   Limit handling to reduce stress for these small, delicate creatures.

### Clear, Concise Answer to the Main Query

To care for a Mediterranean House Gecko, provide a 10-gallon minimum terrarium with climbing decor, maintain temperatures between 75-85°F (24-29°C) with a basking spot, keep humidity at 60-70%, and feed small, gut-loaded insects dusted with calcium and vitamin supplements regularly.

## Understanding Your Mediterranean House Gecko

The Mediterranean House Gecko, known scientifically as *Hemidactylus turcicus*, is a small, nocturnal lizard. People find them across warm regions worldwide, often in or near human dwellings. I find these geckos very charming due to their big eyes and transparent skin. They typically grow to about 4-5 inches, including their tail. Their skin usually shows a pale, almost translucent color with darker spots. This coloration helps them blend into their surroundings.

These geckos have a lifespan of 5-10 years in captivity with proper care. They are generally shy and prefer to hide during the day. At night, they become active, hunting insects. Their specialized toe pads allow them to climb smooth surfaces, even glass. This ability is what makes them so common on house walls and windows. You will notice their quick movements and excellent agility.

### Natural Habitat and Behavior

In the wild, Mediterranean House Geckos live in warm, dry climates. They favor rocky areas, tree bark, and often, human structures. They use cracks and crevices for shelter from predators and harsh weather. They are insectivores, meaning their diet consists primarily of insects. Their nocturnal nature means they hunt after the sun sets. I think their hunting skills are quite impressive for such small creatures. They rely on their excellent night vision to spot prey.

They are relatively solitary animals. While you might see several in one area, they do not form social groups. Observing them in their natural or simulated habitat reveals their keen senses. They are very alert to their surroundings. Providing similar conditions in captivity helps them feel secure. This leads to a healthier and less stressed pet.

### Wild-Caught vs. Captive-Bred Geckos

Most Mediterranean House Geckos available as pets are wild-caught. This means they were taken from their natural environments. Wild-caught geckos can carry parasites or diseases. They might also be very stressed by capture and handling. You may notice they are much more skittish. I always recommend seeking captive-bred animals if possible. Captive-bred geckos are healthier. They adapt to a home environment more easily. They are also less likely to carry illnesses.

If you find a gecko in your home, it is a wild animal. It might be stressed or carry pathogens. Handling wild animals can be risky. If you have an unwanted wild gecko in your house, there are humane methods to remove it. Releasing wild-caught geckos into a new area can disrupt local ecosystems. It is best to avoid keeping wild-caught geckos as pets if you do not know their health status.

## Creating the Perfect Home: Enclosure Setup

Setting up the right home for your Mediterranean House Gecko is crucial for its well-being. A suitable enclosure creates a safe and stimulating environment. I find that careful planning here prevents many common issues later. The goal is to mimic their natural habitat as closely as possible. This includes providing places to hide, climb, and feel secure. A well-designed terrarium is not just a container; it is their little world.

### Choosing the Right Terrarium

For a single Mediterranean House Gecko, a 10-gallon glass terrarium is a good starting point. A 12x12x18 inch (length x width x height) enclosure provides ample climbing space. You can use a taller tank as these geckos love to climb. The enclosure must have a secure, screen top. This top allows for proper ventilation. It also prevents escapes. These geckos are very good at squeezing through small gaps. I always double-check the lid for any potential escape routes.

Consider a front-opening terrarium. This design makes cleaning and feeding easier. It also causes less stress to the gecko. You do not need to reach in from the top. A secure latch is important for all terrariums. Choose a location for the terrarium that is out of direct sunlight. Avoid drafts and high-traffic areas. Stability and safety are key for the enclosure's placement.

### Substrate Choices and Maintenance

The substrate is the material at the bottom of the terrarium. Good substrate choices hold humidity and are safe for geckos. I find that a mixture of coco fiber and sphagnum moss works well. These materials hold moisture. This helps maintain the required humidity levels. Peat moss or chemical-free potting soil are also options. You should avoid sand as a primary substrate. Sand can cause impaction if ingested. Paper towels or reptile carpet are simple alternatives. They are easy to clean but do not retain humidity well.

Regardless of the substrate, spot-clean daily to remove waste. Replace the entire substrate every 2-3 months. This prevents bacteria buildup. Proper substrate maintenance keeps the environment clean. A clean enclosure reduces the risk of illness. When changing substrate, ensure all decor items are clean too. A healthy environment starts with a clean base.

### Decor and Hiding Spots

Mediterranean House Geckos need plenty of places to hide. They are nocturnal. Hiding spots help them feel safe during the day. Provide several cork bark flats, small caves, or hollow logs. I like to offer a variety of textures and shapes. This adds visual appeal and functional space. Arrange decor items to create climbing opportunities. Branches, vines, and artificial plants are excellent for this. Ensure all decor is secure. Heavy items should rest on the terrarium bottom, not on the substrate. This prevents them from falling and harming your gecko.

A shallow water dish is also essential. Use a heavy, non-tip dish. Provide fresh, clean water daily. Some geckos prefer to drink mist droplets. You can mist one side of the enclosure. This gives them another hydration source. Live plants can also add to the decor. Choose reptile-safe plants. Make sure they do not have sharp edges or thorns. Plants like pothos or bromeliads can add natural beauty. They also help maintain humidity.

## Essential Environmental Conditions: Heat, Light, and Humidity

Maintaining proper environmental conditions is vital for your Mediterranean House Gecko's health. These geckos rely on external sources to regulate their body temperature. They also need specific light cycles and humidity levels. I often compare this to setting up a miniature climate zone. Getting these factors right prevents many common health issues. Incorrect temperatures or humidity can quickly cause stress and illness.

### Temperature Management

Mediterranean House Geckos need a temperature gradient. This means different temperature zones within their enclosure. The ambient temperature should be between 75-85°F (24-29°C). Create a basking spot that reaches around 90°F (32°C). This allows your gecko to warm up as needed. A heat mat under one side of the terrarium works well for ambient heat. An overhead ceramic heat emitter can provide the basking spot. These devices emit heat without light. This is important for nocturnal animals. Use a thermostat to control heating devices. This prevents overheating. Overheating can be very dangerous.

Nighttime temperatures can drop slightly. A range of 70-75°F (21-24°C) is acceptable. Avoid extreme drops below 65°F (18°C). Always use thermometers inside the enclosure. Place them at both the warm and cool ends. This helps you monitor the gradient effectively. I find digital thermometers with probes are the most accurate. Consistent temperature management is key to their digestive health and overall well-being.

### Lighting Needs: UVB and Beyond

As nocturnal animals, Mediterranean House Geckos do not require intense lighting. However, they still benefit from a proper light cycle. A low-wattage LED light can provide a day/night cycle. I run this light for 12 hours during the day and turn it off at night. This mimics natural conditions. While some reptile keepers debate UVB for nocturnal geckos, I recommend providing a low-level UVB light. A 2.0 or 5.0 UVB bulb can be beneficial. It helps with calcium absorption. This prevents metabolic bone disease. Mount the UVB bulb outside the screen top. Replace the bulb every 6-12 months. UVB output decreases over time.

Do not use heat lamps with colored bulbs (red, blue). These lights disrupt their natural sleep patterns. Your gecko needs complete darkness at night. This helps them stay active and healthy. Ensure any lighting is placed safely. It should not be accessible for the gecko to touch. Too much direct heat from a light source can burn them. A simple timer can manage the light cycle automatically. This ensures consistency even if you are not home.

### Maintaining Optimal Humidity

Mediterranean House Geckos need moderate humidity levels. Aim for 60-70% humidity. You can achieve this by misting the enclosure once or twice daily. Use a spray bottle with dechlorinated water. Mist one side of the terrarium. Do not make the entire substrate soaking wet. This can cause mold growth. A substrate that holds moisture, like coco fiber, helps maintain humidity. You can also use a humid hide. This is a small container with damp sphagnum moss. Your gecko can retreat there when it needs extra moisture.

A hygrometer is essential for monitoring humidity. Place it inside the terrarium. Check the levels regularly. Proper humidity aids in shedding. It prevents dehydration. If humidity is too low, geckos can have trouble shedding their skin. Stuck shed can lead to health problems. Good ventilation is also important. This prevents stagnant air and respiratory issues. A screen top helps with air circulation. Striking the right balance between misting and ventilation is crucial. ## Feeding Your Gecko: A Balanced Diet

A proper diet is fundamental to the health and longevity of your Mediterranean House Gecko. They are insectivores, meaning they eat insects. Providing a varied diet ensures they get all necessary nutrients. I find that offering different types of prey keeps them interested. It also prevents nutritional deficiencies. Always remember that the health of your gecko depends on the health of its food.

### Staple Insects and Variety

The main part of your gecko's diet should be small, live insects. Crickets are a common staple. Choose crickets that are no larger than the space between your gecko's eyes. This prevents choking. Other excellent options include:
*   **Small Dubia roaches:** These are nutritious and easy to keep.
*   **Mealworms:** Offer these sparingly, as they have a harder exoskeleton.
*   **Black soldier fly larvae (BSFL):** These are high in calcium and very beneficial.
*   **Waxworms:** Offer these as an occasional treat due to their high-fat content.

I try to offer a variety of these insects. This mimics what they might eat in the wild. Avoid feeding wild-caught insects. These can carry parasites or pesticides. Feed your adult gecko 3-5 appropriately sized insects every 2-3 days. Younger geckos need to eat daily. Monitor their weight and activity. Adjust feeding frequency as needed. Some geckos may refuse certain insects. Observe their preferences.

### Gut-Loading and Supplementation

Gut-loading means feeding nutritious food to the feeder insects. This transfers the nutrients to your gecko. I gut-load crickets and roaches for at least 24 hours before feeding. Use fresh vegetables like carrots, sweet potatoes, or dark leafy greens. Commercial gut-loading diets are also available. This practice significantly improves the nutritional value of their meal.

In addition to gut-loading, you must dust feeder insects with supplements.
*   **Calcium with D3:** Dust insects with this supplement 3-4 times a week for young geckos. For adults, 2-3 times a week is often enough. D3 helps geckos absorb calcium. Without enough D3, they cannot properly use calcium.
*   **Multivitamin:** Dust insects with a reptile multivitamin once every 1-2 weeks. This ensures they get essential vitamins and trace minerals.

Simply place the insects in a plastic bag with a small amount of supplement powder. Gently shake the bag to coat them. Then, offer them to your gecko. Proper supplementation prevents metabolic bone disease. This is a common and serious illness in reptiles. It results from a lack of calcium and D3. You must ensure all insects are appropriately supplemented.

### Water and Hydration

Always provide a shallow, clean water dish. Change the water daily. Use dechlorinated water. Geckos may also drink water droplets from misting. I mist the enclosure daily or every other day. This provides additional hydration. It also helps maintain humidity. Ensure there are always multiple sources of water. Dehydration can quickly lead to health problems. It is a quiet killer for reptiles.

Watch your gecko for signs of dehydration. These signs include sunken eyes or wrinkled skin. If you suspect dehydration, increase misting. You might also offer water from a dropper. This helps them rehydrate quickly. Proper hydration supports all bodily functions. It helps with digestion and shedding.

## Health and Wellness: Common Issues and Prevention

Keeping your Mediterranean House Gecko healthy involves regular observation and preventative care. These geckos are generally hardy, but they can face specific health challenges. I believe that being proactive is the best approach to pet health. Understanding common issues helps you act quickly if problems arise. Many issues are preventable with proper husbandry.

### Recognizing Healthy Behavior

A healthy Mediterranean House Gecko is active and alert at night. It will have clear eyes and a clean body. Its tail should be plump, indicating good fat reserves. I look for smooth, healthy skin with no signs of stuck shed. They should have a good appetite. Regular bowel movements are also a positive sign. They will usually retreat to their hides during the day. This is their normal, nocturnal pattern.

Lethargy, refusing food, or unusual posture are red flags. If your gecko is always out during the day, it might be stressed. Weight loss can indicate underlying health problems. Swollen joints or a crooked spine point to metabolic bone disease. Observing your gecko daily helps you learn its normal behavior. This makes it easier to spot changes quickly. Early detection is key to successful treatment.

### Addressing Common Health Concerns

**Stuck Shed:** This is a common issue. It happens when humidity is too low. Skin can get stuck around toes or eyes. If left untreated, it can lead to constricted toes or vision problems.
*   **Prevention:** Maintain proper humidity. Provide a humid hide.
*   **Treatment:** Give your gecko a shallow, lukewarm water bath. Place it in a small container with damp paper towels. The humidity will loosen the skin. Gently remove any loose pieces with a cotton swab. Do not pull hard.

**Metabolic Bone Disease (MBD):** This serious condition results from a lack of calcium and/or D3. Bones become weak and soft. Symptoms include tremors, lethargy, swollen joints, and a rubbery jaw.
*   **Prevention:** Proper calcium and D3 supplementation. Provide low-level UVB lighting.
*   **Treatment:** Immediate veterinary attention is crucial. The vet may prescribe calcium injections or special diets. MBD is treatable, but often irreversible damage occurs.

**Parasites:** Wild-caught geckos are more prone to parasites. Internal parasites can cause weight loss, lethargy, and abnormal stools. External parasites like mites can cause skin irritation.
*   **Prevention:** Acquire captive-bred geckos. Quarantine new animals.
*   **Treatment:** A reptile vet can diagnose and treat parasites. Fecal tests detect internal parasites.

**Dehydration:** This can occur if water is not available or if humidity is too low.
*   **Prevention:** Fresh water daily. Consistent misting.
*   **Treatment:** Increase misting. Offer water from a dropper. Seek vet care for severe dehydration.

## Handling and Enrichment: Building a Bond

Mediterranean House Geckos are small and delicate. They are not pets that typically enjoy extensive handling. Their small size makes them fragile. Their quick movements make them prone to escape. I always prioritize their comfort and safety over my desire to hold them. However, you can still build a gentle connection and enrich their lives.

### Gentle Handling Techniques

Limit handling to only when necessary, such as for enclosure cleaning or health checks. When you need to handle them, be gentle and deliberate. Do not grab them by their tails. Their tails can detach as a defense mechanism. This is called autotomy. While a new tail will regrow, it will not look the same. It is a stressful experience for the gecko.

To pick up your gecko, gently coax it onto your hand. Let it walk onto your palm. Use both hands as a "safety net" in case it jumps. Keep your movements slow and steady. Provide a secure environment for handling. Sit on the floor to minimize the distance if it falls. Observe for signs of stress, such as frantic running or tail wiggling. If you notice these, return your gecko to its enclosure. The goal is to make handling as brief and stress-free as possible for the gecko.

### Enriching Their Environment

Even though they are small, Mediterranean House Geckos benefit from environmental enrichment. Enrichment stimulates their natural behaviors. It keeps them mentally and physically active. I find that a diverse enclosure prevents boredom. It encourages exploration.

Here are ways to enrich their environment:
*   **Varied climbing structures:** Offer branches, cork bark, and vines at different angles. This provides new routes for climbing.
*   **Multiple hiding spots:** Offer a choice of hides in various locations. This gives them options for resting and feeling secure.
*   **Live plants:** Incorporate reptile-safe live plants. They add texture and visual interest. They also provide extra cover.
*   **Rearrange decor:** Occasionally, I will slightly rearrange the decor. This creates a "new" environment for them to explore. Do not change it too often. Small changes are best.
*   **Live feeder insects:** Allowing them to hunt live insects provides natural stimulation. This is much better than offering dead insects.

You should remember that these geckos are quite shy. They prefer to explore on their own terms. Do not force interaction. A rich and stimulating habitat is the best form of interaction for them. It helps them live a full and healthy life. ## Breeding Mediterranean House Geckos

Breeding Mediterranean House Geckos can be a rewarding experience. It helps expand the captive-bred population. This reduces the demand for wild-caught animals. However, breeding requires extra commitment. You must provide specific conditions. You also need to care for the eggs and hatchlings. I find it fascinating to watch the complete life cycle.

### Recognizing Breeding Readiness

Mediterranean House Geckos usually reach sexual maturity around 6-12 months of age. Females will appear plumper when carrying eggs. You might even see the outlines of eggs through their translucent skin. Males are generally smaller than females. They also have prominent pores on their underside. These are pre-anal pores. It is important to know the sex of your geckos. This helps prevent unwanted breeding.

A healthy pair will naturally attempt to breed. Provide proper diet and environmental conditions. This includes a consistent temperature gradient and humidity. A breeding pair needs a slightly larger enclosure. This provides more space for each gecko. Ensure the female has enough calcium. She will use a lot of calcium for egg production. Insufficient calcium can lead to egg binding. This is a serious health issue.

### Caring for Eggs and Hatchlings

Female Mediterranean House Geckos lay clutches of 1-2 eggs. They usually lay them in secure, damp locations. They will often bury them in the substrate or hide them in crevices. You should provide a dedicated egg-laying site. A small container with damp sphagnum moss works well. Once laid, carefully remove the eggs. Place them in an incubator. Do not rotate the eggs. This can harm the developing embryo.

Incubate eggs at around 80-85°F (27-29°C) with high humidity (80-90%). A mixture of perlite or vermiculite, moistened, can be used as an incubation medium. Hatching typically occurs in 60-90 days. Hatchlings are tiny and very delicate. They need their own small enclosures. A shoebox-sized container with paper towel substrate is suitable. Provide small hides and a shallow water dish. Feed them small feeder insects, like pinhead crickets or fruit flies. Dust their food with calcium and vitamins. Caring for hatchlings requires careful attention. They are more vulnerable to dehydration and nutritional deficiencies.
